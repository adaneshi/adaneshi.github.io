Conditional Value of Edge AI for U.S. Federal Agencies

Ashkaan Daneshi

Columbia University

Federal agencies publish annual inventories of the AI systems they use. Each system can run at the edge, at a remote site, or on both.

The Question

Since 2023 the Office of Management and Budget has published inventories in which federal agencies list the AI systems they use or plan to use. A camera at a border crossing, a tool used by a benefits clerk, or a system on a vehicle can send each request to a remote data center, or it can answer the request at the edge. Here, I ask when edge AI may have a useful advantage over remote AI, and then whether the inventories describe many systems for which the choice matters.

A Queueing Model

The edge avoids the time a request spends on the network, but it is slower than a remote server, so requests wait in a queue as soon as it is busy. I model each site as a queue and use published hardware values for the speed of each computer and the delay of the network. When the two sites are compared at the same share of busy time, they respond equally fast when each is busy about 70 percent of the time.

The more relevant comparison gives both sites the same flow of requests. Since the edge is slower, it becomes busy sooner, and it loses its advantage once it is busy about 55 percent of the time. For the slowest requests, measured by the 95th percentile of response time, the remote site is faster at all but the lightest loads, and the edge also falls behind when requests arrive in surges.

Requests are answered either at the edge, with a short network delay and a slower processor, or at the remote site, with a longer network delay and a faster processor.

The Inventories

The three public inventories cover 2023, 2024, and 2025, and the start dates they record reach back to 2016. Each row gives the name of a use case, a short description, and its stage, such as planned, pilot, or in use. The inventories do not record how busy a computer is or how long the network delay is, so the choice of site cannot be read from them directly. I therefore screen the descriptions for the conditions associated with local processing, namely field equipment, poor connectivity, a need for an immediate answer, and processing on the device. Between two and four percent of filings meet the screen in each year, with no detectable change over time.

Use cases marked as affecting rights or safety are concentrated in security agencies, which handle policing, borders, defense, and veterans' benefits. In 2024 about half of their filings carry that label under a broad coding, against about three percent at other agencies. In 2024 and 2025, however, security agencies describe the conditions of local processing about as often as other agencies do.

Movements of use cases between stages from the 2024 inventory to the 2025 inventory. Each number is the share of use cases in the stage of origin, and the dashed arrows lead to names that do not appear in the later inventory.

Use cases not in use in 2024. Of those that can be matched in 2025, 141 are in use and 485 are not, and 473 cannot be matched. The share that reached use lies between 0.128, if no unmatched case reached use, and 0.559, if every unmatched case did.

From One Inventory to the Next

Of the 626 use cases that were not in use in 2024 and can be matched in 2025, 141 were in use a year later. Nineteen of the 626 meet the screen for local processing, and one of these nineteen was in use in 2025, against about one in four among the other matched use cases. The descriptions that point to local processing are therefore not the ones that move into use, although nineteen cases are too few for a precise comparison.
