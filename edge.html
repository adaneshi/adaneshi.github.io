Public AI, nearby or far away

Ashkaan Daneshi

Columbia University

Since 2023 the Office of Management and Budget has published the lists federal agencies file of the AI systems they say they are using. I use those lists to work out when the computation should sit next to the work, and whether the filings that describe field conditions are the ones later marked in use.

The public circle

Where this sits

I place this paper in the public circle of the diagram on the home page. Firms still do most of the making of goods, and government buys from them, regulates them, and operates services of its own. The only evidence in the paper is the lists those agencies file. Payments, identity systems, and the power grid, which require private capital and public authority and public trust at the same time, lie further along than these lists can reach.

Security, in this paper, is a form of public production the government has to direct, covering policing, borders, the administration of benefits, and the agencies that file those cases. What I then look for is whether that direction coincides with a practical choice about where the computer sits.

A short wire or a fast machine

A request can be answered in two ways. A smaller machine can sit next to the camera, the clerk, or the vehicle, in which case the wire is short and the reply begins to come back at once, though the machine is slow and a queue builds as soon as it is busy. A larger machine in a distant hall works through requests faster, and every request pays for the time spent on the wire.

When the nearby machine is idle, the short wire is the faster arrangement. Once that machine is busy most of the time, requests wait in its queue, and the distant machine is quicker even after the trip. On the benchmark hardware used in the paper, the two response times are equal when the nearby machine is busy about seventy percent of the time. If the quantity of interest is the slow replies, rather than the average reply, the distant machine is ahead from a much lower load: the nearby machine shortens the typical request, while a request that arrives into a queue that has already formed still waits.

Some requests stay nearby, on a short wire and a slower machine. The rest travel to a faster machine.

Waiting time for a request. In the left panel the two machines are equally busy, and their waiting times meet near seventy percent. In the middle panel they face the same flow of work, so the nearby machine falls behind sooner, because that flow crowds it. The right panel is the slow replies, and there the distant machine is ahead from early on.

What the lists record

The Office of Management and Budget has released three of these lists, covering 2023, 2024, and 2025. Each row is a form an agency filled in, with a name, a short description, and a stage such as planned, pilot, or in use. The form does not record how busy a machine is, how long the wire is, or whether anyone observed the system in operation, so a longer list is a larger stack of forms.

I score the descriptions for language that would make a nearby machine the relevant arrangement: equipment used in the field, a connection that is dead or thin, a requirement that the answer come back at once, and computation carried out on the device rather than in a remote hall. Few rows contain more than one of those. The share is a few percent in each year, and it is a little higher in 2023 than in the later lists.

Agencies that handle policing, borders, defense, and veterans' benefits file a much larger share of the cases marked as touching rights or safety. In the 2024 list that share is about half of their cases, against a few percent among the other agencies. Those same descriptions seldom mention field equipment or a requirement for an immediate answer, so the security classification of a filing and the practical case for computing nearby appear as separate patterns in the forms.

Follow a name from 2024 into 2025. The dashed arrows are names that do not appear again. The step from planned or pilot into use is a thin one.

Among 2024 cases that were not yet in use, 141 that I can match are in use in 2025. Another 473 names vanish. If all of those vanished names were in use, the share would be a bit over half. If none of them were, it would be about one in eight.

What follows a year later

Among the 2024 cases I can still find in 2025, the descriptions that read as nearby and time-critical work are rarely the cases marked in use. Nineteen of the matched cases that were not yet in use met that description, and one of those nineteen is in use a year later, against about one in five of the other matched cases. The wording of the filing and the stage recorded a year later move separately.

Joining 2023 to 2024 is harder. Of 709 names that were not already retired, 472 do not appear again.

The public draft is SSRN 7141719. The 2025 list lengthens the record of what agencies wrote down. The comparison of the two machines comes from the benchmark hardware, which the lists do not contain, and the additional year leaves that comparison where it was.
